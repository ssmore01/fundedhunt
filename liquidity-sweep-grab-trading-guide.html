Liquidity Sweeps and Grabs Explained — Why the Market Takes Your Stop Loss Before Moving Your Way

📅 July 15, 2026⏱ 14 min read✍️ FundedHunt

You set your stop loss at what looks like a perfect level. The market comes down, takes your stop, and then immediately reverses and moves exactly where you predicted. You were right about the direction but wrong at the worst possible moment.

This happens to almost every retail trader, repeatedly, until they understand one concept: liquidity. Once you understand what liquidity is, why it pools at certain price levels, and how smart money hunts it, your entire view of the market changes.

What is Liquidity in Trading?

Liquidity is the ability to buy or sell an asset without causing a significant move in price. A highly liquid market has plenty of buyers and sellers at any given moment, so large orders can be filled without the price moving dramatically.

Here is what most retail traders never think about. When a large institution wants to buy 10,000 lots of Gold, they cannot just click buy and expect the order to fill at one price. That order is so large that filling it all at once would push the price significantly against them. They need enough opposing orders already sitting in the market to absorb their position. And those orders live at very specific places on the chart.

The core idea: Large institutions need opposing orders to fill their positions without moving price against themselves. They engineer situations where enough of those orders exist. Understanding where those orders pool is the foundation of liquidity trading.

Where Does Liquidity Live on a Chart?

Liquidity pools at predictable locations because retail traders, taught by the same textbooks and YouTube channels, place their stop losses and pending orders at the same types of levels.

Above Swing Highs — Buy Side Liquidity

Traders who are short place their stop loss just above clear swing highs. A cluster of buy stop orders sits above every notable high on the chart. That cluster is buy side liquidity (BSL).

Below Swing Lows — Sell Side Liquidity

Traders who are long place their stop loss just below swing lows. A cluster of sell stop orders sits below every notable low. That cluster is sell side liquidity (SSL).

Equal Highs and Equal Lows

When price forms two or more highs at the same level, or two or more lows at the same level, every retail trader who sees a double top or double bottom places their stop just beyond those levels. The more obvious the level, the more stop losses are stacked there.

Previous Day, Week and Session Highs and Lows

The high and low of the previous trading day, the previous week, or the Asian session are areas where algorithmic systems and institutional traders consistently place pending orders. These levels are reliable liquidity targets.

What is a Liquidity Sweep?

A liquidity sweep happens when price moves beyond a key level, triggers the stop losses and pending orders sitting there, and then reverses back.

When price sweeps above a swing high, the stop losses of short traders get triggered, converting their positions into buy orders. Breakout traders' pending buy stops also get triggered. Suddenly there is a flood of buy orders at that level. Institutions who want to sell now have a massive pool of buyers to sell into. They fill their short positions using all those triggered orders, and then price reverses downward.

From the outside, it looks like the market faked out above the high and reversed. In reality, it is a completely logical process of institutions using retail stop losses as the opposing liquidity they need to fill large positions.

What is a Liquidity Grab?

A liquidity grab is the same concept as a sweep but typically refers to a faster, more aggressive version of the move. The key visual clue is a long wick on a candlestick that pierces a key level and closes back inside the range. The wick is the evidence of the grab. Price went there, collected the orders, and returned.

Visual clue: A long wick that pierces a swing high or low and closes back inside the previous range is one of the clearest signals of a liquidity grab. The close back inside the range is your confirmation.

How to Plan Your Trade Around a Liquidity Sweep

Here is a complete step by step approach for building a trade around this concept.

Step 1 — Identify the liquidity pool on H4 or H1. Find your key swing high or swing low and mark the exact level. Note whether it is buy side liquidity above a high, or sell side liquidity below a low.

Step 2 — Establish your higher timeframe bias. If the overall structure on H4 is bullish, you are primarily looking for sweeps of sell side liquidity below lows which give you long entries. If bearish, you want sweeps of buy side liquidity above highs which give you short entries.

Step 3 — Wait for price to pierce the level. Do not enter before the sweep. Watch price approach the level. When it pierces the high or low you marked, the sweep is potentially in progress.

Step 4 — Look for confirmation of the reversal. After the sweep, look for a strong bearish candle closing back below the swept high (for a short entry) or a strong bullish candle closing back above the swept low (for a long entry). A Break of Structure on M5 in the opposite direction is a strong confirmation signal.

Step 5 — Enter on the confirmation, not the sweep. Enter your trade after the confirmation candle closes. Not during the sweep, not on the wick, but after you have evidence that the reversal is underway.

Step 6 — Place your stop loss beyond the sweep extreme. Place your stop a few points beyond the highest point of the sweep candle for shorts, or the lowest point for longs. Not at the swept level itself but beyond the actual extreme of the wick.

Step 7 — Target the opposing liquidity pool. Your take profit should be the next major liquidity pool on the other side of the market. Let price travel from one pool to the next.

Common Mistakes When Trading Liquidity

  • Entering before the sweep happens. You see the level and short early. Price sweeps through your stop before reversing. You were right about direction but wrong about timing.
  • Confusing a genuine breakout with a sweep. Not every move above a high is a sweep. If price breaks above and holds, forming new structure, that may be a real breakout. Confirmation is everything.
  • Placing your stop at the swept level rather than beyond it. If price sweeps to $3,323 and you place your stop at $3,320, any minimal revisit stops you out. Place it beyond the extreme of the wick.
  • Ignoring higher timeframe bias. A sweep of a low in a bullish H4 trend is a buying opportunity. The same sweep in a bearish H4 trend is just a pause before continuation down.

Final Thought

The market moves from liquidity pool to liquidity pool. Every significant move on a chart is either running toward a pool of resting orders or reversing after collecting them. Once you see the market this way, the chart starts to tell you a story instead of looking like random noise.

Stop placing your stop losses at the obvious levels where everyone else puts theirs. Start watching what the market does when it reaches those levels. Wait for the sweep, wait for the confirmation, and then enter with the institutions rather than becoming the liquidity they need to fill their positions.
